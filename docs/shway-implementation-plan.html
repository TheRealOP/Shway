Creative intent interpreter

Shway: hackathon build plan

Prepared October 10, 2026. Solo builder; submission Sunday morning, October 11; GPU available. Exact deadline and hackathon rules remain to be checked. Three agents reviewed interaction design, architecture, and research scope. Repository: https://github.com/TheRealOP/Shway, forked from storytold/filmcraft at 7bd76212629a067a9d1c5426d751de9faf4664b7. Linear project: https://linear.app/dev-system/project/schway-4deadf7f36cc (existing name Schway retained). The fork has not yet been compiled or functionally tested in this planning session.

Product promise

Express an edit with your voice, hands, and references. See your intention become an editable preview, steer it yourself, and compare how different versions communicate your vision.

The user supplies creative choices. AI translates, offers alternatives, and executes technical operations. A complete result must include a real edited and exported video, not just an animated interface.

Primary workflow clarified by the creator

The creator records several clips/takes of themselves speaking, imports them, and describes what the video should communicate, show, and feel like. AI assembles and plays a first cut, handling routine editing. The creator watches, twists to scrub, gestures to reject or cut an unwanted take, and speaks the reason and desired next beat. The system revises the local edit and learns the preference. Lighting correction and music synchronization are part of the intended experience.

This supersedes the earlier assumption that the creator manually performs every initial cut. Expressive performance controls remain useful for refinement. The user can say “make a first cut” or choose an exploratory mode; automatic assembly does not force creative delegation on every project.

Footage-to-first-cut pipeline

  1. Import and index originals. Transcribe speech with word timings, detect candidate take/shot boundaries, and retain source IDs, ranges, and synchronized audio. Build proxy/sample representations where supported.
  2. Group repeated or alternative statements using transcripts plus audio/video review. Keep originals and rejected takes available; distinguish repetition from a deliberate callback. Recognize uncertainty rather than invent missing speech.
  3. Build an editable story brief from the user's directions: intended message, audience, narrative order, desired feeling, required moments, duration, references, and exclusions. Ask only about consequential ambiguity.
  4. A multimodal model reviews content, delivery, expression, and visual/audio quality to propose a few opening takes. Prioritize relevance to the brief, clarity, and continuity. TRIBE may analyze a shortlist of rendered openings with context; its outputs are exploratory evidence, not a validated hook-quality or engagement score.
  5. Assemble a first cut: select takes, order narrative beats, trim obvious setup/dead space, preserve meaningful pauses, and propose basic audio/visual corrections. Express these as inspectable Filmcraft operations with source provenance. Show and play the actual edited timeline.
  6. During review, a contextual reject gesture targets the current take/selected interval. Voice supplies the reason: “That sounds rehearsed; use the take where I laugh, then show the result.” This requests take replacement/reordering rather than necessarily placing a razor cut. Maintain a distinct split-at-playhead command to avoid ambiguity.
  7. Preview the revision, retain the previous cut for comparison, and accept or undo it as a coherent action. Preserve dialogue continuity, linked audio, project revision, and source range boundaries.
  8. Offer lighting and music refinement, then render/analyze/export the selected version.

The product's creative memory records the rejected source range, reason, replacement preference, and acceptance. It can retrieve “prefer conversational delivery” on later takes. A single correction does not establish a universal preference or justify changing every clip.

Lighting and music

Lighting control starts with exposure, white balance, contrast, and shot-to-shot matching on existing footage. Propose explicit color parameters and let gesture adjustment preview them. Reconstructing actual lighting or generating a relit face is a separate capability and is not promised by ordinary grading.

Music synchronization starts with known/licensed tracks: align selected edits or emphasis points to detected beats, place the desired musical moment at the reveal, and lower music under speech. Preserve intentional pacing when beat alignment conflicts with the brief. User taps may supply a desired rhythm but are optional. Verify beat/onset analysis, audio-gain automation, and required edit commands in the fork before declaring support.

Decisions

  • Fork Filmcraft and pin the starting upstream commit. Record inherited capabilities and the hackathon delta.
  • First try its browser build with a TypeScript interaction surface alongside the editor canvas. Keep Rust editing logic authoritative. Verify this choice through an import/play/edit/undo/export gate before expanding the UI.
  • If browser performance fails that gate, retain the interaction surface and bridge a local service to Filmcraft desktop's JSON control channel. Avoid building both backends beyond a small adapter contract.
  • Camera hand tracking is the selected primary input, with a microphone for creative intent. Seated, small movements; visible engagement control; conventional input remains available. A phone controller is a future input adapter.
  • Use deterministic gesture mappings for live control. Use a multimodal LLM for contextual interpretation at gesture boundaries and voice utterances.
  • Use TRIBE v2 in a separate GPU-backed Python analysis service. Analysis is asynchronous and never blocks editing.
  • Keep interaction state transitions stable. Personalize calibration, parameter sensitivity, and explicit gesture bindings between actions.
  • Use a small feedback-and-replay adaptation loop initially. Full Harness² integration is stretch work. Swirl-Dynamics has no necessary role in this build.

Additional expressive-control demo

  1. Open a scene and say: “Quiet at first, then build toward the reveal. Keep the dialogue clear.”
  2. Draw a rising curve with a hand. The editor stores it as the creator's intended intensity arc and shows the interpretation for correction.
  3. Tap a rhythm in a dedicated recording mode. Taps become proposed cut markers. Preview the cuts before applying them.
  4. Select an interval and say “make this swell like this” while lifting a hand. The UI identifies the interval and displays a proposed music-gain curve. Accept it, then adjust its height directly.
  5. Say “That was too much. Smaller movements should make smaller changes.” The system proposes reduced sensitivity, demonstrates it, and saves the chosen profile.
  6. Compare original and edited playback. Render both versions and show actual TRIBE predictions, clearly labeled as modeled neural responses.
  7. Export the chosen version. Show that the creative actions exist as normal edits and can be undone.

If live TRIBE inference is too slow, display genuine cached outputs for those exact rendered versions with an analysis timestamp. A changed edit invalidates its result; never show old analysis as current.

These are proposed controls, not universal gesture meanings. The same upward movement can mean gain or exposure only after the parameter is explicitly bound. A rising hand should not silently mean “more tension.”

User follow-up: support swipes and use hand twisting as the default scrubbing interaction throughout the interface. Proposed initial mapping: capture a neutral palm orientation when engaging; relative rotation about the forearm axis controls signed shuttle speed, with a dead zone around neutral and a bounded maximum. Neutral pauses, release ends control. In fine mode, use a smaller gain and frame stepping. Avoid integrating frame-to-frame rotation indefinitely, which can drift. Estimate orientation from several hand landmarks; test occlusion, handedness, and practical rotation range. If orientation confidence falls, pause and require re-engagement. Keep precise frame controls available.

Swipes are temporal gestures: recognize deliberate displacement and velocity over a short window, then emit one event with a cooldown. During an active twist adjustment, the same motion must not also trigger navigation. Default swipes navigate; users can explicitly rebind them within a context. Destructive actions require a preview or confirmation state.

A held key or screen control can engage editing when pinch is uncomfortable or ambiguous. Release ends continuous adjustment. Mouse, keyboard, and a clear cancel action remain available. Low tracking confidence pauses control; it never guesses a destructive edit. Playback audio should not become a voice instruction: start with push-to-talk and record-mode feedback.

Technical flow

Use asynchronous external speech recognition and import word timings through Filmcraft's documented transcript.set command. Built-in Whisper is optional and unavailable in the browser build. Keep the source media index distinct from timeline clip IDs; avoid relying on parent-media transcript inheritance through subclips. Convert source/media-time keyframes and sequence-time positions explicitly at the adapter boundary.

Core records: StoryBrief, MediaIndex, InteractionContext, EditProposal, GestureProfile, CreativePreferences, ProjectVersion, and AnalysisJob. The edit proposal is inspectable data, not only hidden prompt text. Validate a replacement's available duration: an upstream take-replacement command may retain timeline duration and hold a final frame when the new source is too short. Preview or re-plan duration rather than silently accepting a frozen ending.

Camera and voice → synchronized observations → intent interpretation → interaction state and validated edit proposal → Filmcraft commands → preview and commit → feedback history.

Rendered version → TRIBE worker → timestamped neural-response results → comparison panel.

Maintain a fast path for tracking, smoothing, state transitions, and parameter updates. Keep LLM calls off this path. MediaPipe can run in a worker so synchronous video recognition does not compete with editor rendering.

The initial intent record contains: selected clip IDs and interval; utterance; timestamped hand trajectory; candidate parameter; desired curve or rhythm; confidence; constraints such as preserve dialogue; and whether each interpretation was supplied by the user, confirmed, or inferred.

For gesture/voice fusion, associate “this” and “like that” with selection and movement in a short bounded time window. Carry a screen snapshot or small gesture recording only when useful and explicitly enabled. Derived landmarks are enough for many controls; they do not explain a gesture's emotional meaning by themselves.

An edit proposal contains explicit targets, bounded parameters, expected project revision, and supported Filmcraft command IDs. Reject stale proposals. Discover available commands at runtime rather than relying on guessed command names. Verify one real command for every demonstrated control.

Finite-state transducer and continuous control

Proposed states: Idle → Targeting → Armed → Adjusting → Preview → Committed, plus Cancelled and TrackingLost.

The transducer maps discrete observations and current state to actions: select a clip, bind a parameter, start a preview, commit, cancel. Numeric hand trajectories live in a separate continuous controller. This combination is a practical finite-state interaction system with numeric context, rather than a pure FST that understands arbitrary motion.

One performed adjustment must be one understandable undo action. Verify Filmcraft's grouping facilities; if the necessary transaction is unavailable, add a small Rust preview/commit interface. Do not assume command batches are atomic or stream hundreds of independent edits into history. On tracking loss, preserve the pre-action state and require explicit re-engagement.

TRIBE's two proposed uses

Understanding creative vision: capture vision through speech, movement, selected references, the intended arc, and user preference comparisons. A multimodal LLM turns these into an editable creative brief. TRIBE does not decode the creator's private intention from muscle activity or a hand trajectory.

As a later experiment, analyze reference clips the creator likes and compare their predicted response patterns with candidate edits under a consistent protocol. Treat resemblance as an exploratory signal, not an emotion label or proof that two videos mean the same thing.

Analyzing the edit: run TRIBE on the actual rendered audio/video. Show predicted cortical response over time, an optional brain visualization, and A/B differences. Label any scalar aggregate by its mathematical definition; do not relabel average activation as engagement, suspense, or quality.

The upstream wrapper predicts an average subject and documents a five-second timing offset. Inspect its returned time alignment and test it before drawing results on the timeline; avoid compensating twice. Preserve surrounding context when comparing edited segments, because sequence history and changed durations matter. Store version/content hash, source timeline mapping, model version, preprocessing, and analysis timestamps. Measure actual GPU memory use and runtime on the demo clip early.

Desired suspense, comprehension, or recall must be evaluated separately. Ask a few viewers to mark the perceived reveal, rate the intended feeling, or answer a content question. Show their responses separately from model predictions. Neural encoding predictions do not guarantee an emotional effect or establish causality.

Adaptation

Log the proposed action, accepted action, explicit correction, and subsequent undo. An undo alone is ambiguous: it may indicate a creative preference, recognition failure, or exploration.

Demonstrate one learned preference: smaller vertical movements produce less gain change. Store a versioned user profile. A contextual binding can also be learned from an explicit correction such as “In audio mode, this gesture means volume.”

Gesture personalization is core scope, not only a later feature. In calibration, collect comfortable examples of pinch, swipe, and twist, including neutral/rest movements. Adapt handedness, rotation range, dead zones, activation thresholds, smoothing, and parameter gain. Recognition learning and command binding are separate: a recognizer identifies the user's gesture; a contextual profile determines what that gesture does. Explicit corrections can change a binding; an isolated undo does not silently retrain it. Freeze a profile during an active gesture and switch versions only between actions. Demonstrate an actual before/after correction and successful reuse.

An LLM proposes a structured profile patch. Validate allowed fields, replay recorded gestures, compare task success, accidental activations, and latency, and present the change between actions. Keep a resettable baseline. Interaction transitions, target boundaries, and cancel behavior remain predictable.

Harness² is relevant as a later framework for improving the intent interpreter's prompts and tool guidance. Its documented execution framework is not a ready-made gesture learner. An adapted Propose–Probe–Compose loop can test candidates on sandbox projects with explicit user feedback and deterministic checks. Do not let it rewrite the active editor while the user is performing an edit.

For gesture adaptation, propose a bounded profile patch, probe it on recorded gesture traces and sandbox edit tasks, then compose/adopt it when recognition, false-trigger, and task-success checks pass and the user accepts a changed command meaning. This can initially be a small Harness²-inspired loop; identify it accurately as inspired by the framework unless the actual framework is integrated. Swirl-Dynamics (if this is the intended “spiral” reference) does not supply an out-of-the-box personalized gesture recognizer. A temporal classifier or calibrated trajectory rules are the direct recognition tools.

Acceptance checks

  • A performed gesture produces actual timeline/effect changes that survive export.
  • One continuous gesture creates one undoable operation; cancel restores the starting state.
  • Resting hands, lost tracking, repeated frames, and unrelated speech do not trigger edits.
  • A voice instruction is associated with the intended target; uncertain targets get an explicit selection step.
  • LLM proposals cannot execute unsupported commands, invalid values, or stale clip references.
  • Gesture-to-visible-feedback latency goal: p95 below 150 ms on demo hardware, measured rather than claimed. LLM and TRIBE latency are reported separately.
  • Replay tests cover selection, adjustment, cancellation, tracking loss, correction, and reset. Run relevant upstream checks for modified Rust code.
  • Personalization improves the demonstrated mapping and does not increase accidental activation on held-out traces.
  • Analysis panels clearly distinguish user intent, measurable edit properties, model predictions, and actual viewer responses.

Input alternative under evaluation: acoustic desk controls

The creator of Perimeter ↗ describes four calibrated desk tap zones recognized from the laptop microphone, local processing, and a macOS application. They report surface dependence and sensitivity issues. The post invites requests for the repository but does not expose an implementation or license to inspect. These are creator reports, not independently verified performance.

This is a future alternative; camera hand tracking has been selected as the primary input. Audio tap classification can potentially provide spatially distinct events and rhythm; it does not establish reliable continuous hand position, airborne gesture recognition, or force measurement.

Proposed product interaction: say “Record the pacing,” then perform beats on a calibrated desk zone during playback. Detected onsets become proposed timeline markers. Another zone can audition; another can cancel or undo. Explicit modes prevent each tap from simultaneously becoming a beat and a command. Keep direct curve drawing on the trackpad as the initial continuous input if this path is selected.

Feasibility experiment: calibrate two zones first, then test unseen taps at different strengths, typing, moving a cup, speech, and the actual soundtrack. Use headphones to remove speaker playback from the first trial, then test speakers separately. Measure per-zone recognition, false triggers, missed taps, and timestamp error. Unknown events should be rejected. Recalibrate after moving the laptop. If zoning is unreliable, evaluate a single rhythm pad rather than pretending it is a coordinate tracker.

Possible implementation approach, not a claim about Perimeter's code: transient detection, short audio-window features, a per-desk classifier, and a reject threshold. Keep tap detection local; voice interpretation is a separate explicitly engaged path. Prefer acoustic timing measurements over an LLM for beat placement. Correct for measured pipeline delay when mapping tap timestamps to playback time.

This alternative could change the demo from conducting in the air to physically performing an edit on the desk. Its value should be tested by whether it captures a rhythm or phrase more naturally than clicking timeline markers.
